# Logical Fallacies

A **logical fallacy** is an error in reasoning that makes an argument weak or misleading, even if it sounds convincing. Spotting fallacies helps you judge ads, speeches, social media posts and your own essays.

## Common fallacies

- **Ad hominem (attacking the person)**: attacking the speaker instead of the argument. *Don't listen to her plan for the canteen; she can't even keep her desk clean.*
- **Straw man**: twisting the other side's argument into a weaker version and attacking that. *A: We should have fewer assignments. B: So you want students to do nothing all day?*
- **Hasty generalization**: a big conclusion from too few examples. *My two classmates from that school are rude, so all students there are rude.*
- **False dilemma (either-or)**: presenting only two choices when more exist. *Either you join the varsity team or you don't care about the school.*
- **Slippery slope**: claiming one small step will lead to extreme results without proof. *If we allow phones at lunch, soon everyone will fail their exams.*
- **Bandwagon**: something is true or good because many people believe or do it. *Everyone is buying this phone, so it must be the best.*
- **Appeal to false authority**: using a famous person who is not an expert on the topic. *A singer says this medicine cures colds, so it works.*
- **Red herring**: changing the topic to distract from the real issue. *Why worry about the flooding? Look at how beautiful the new park is.*
- **Circular reasoning (begging the question)**: the conclusion is used as the proof. *He is trustworthy because he is honest.*
- **False cause (post hoc)**: assuming that because B came after A, A caused B. *I wore my lucky socks and passed, so the socks made me pass.*
- **Appeal to emotion**: using fear, pity or pride instead of evidence. *Think of the poor puppies — buy our brand of dog food.*

## How to respond to a fallacy

1. Name what the argument is really claiming.
2. Ask what evidence actually supports it.
3. Point out the gap politely: *That's about the speaker, not the plan* or *There are more than two options here.*

In your own writing, avoid fallacies by using enough evidence, representing other views fairly and keeping to the issue.
