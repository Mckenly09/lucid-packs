# Poetry and Drama

## Elements of poetry

A **poem** uses carefully chosen words, sound and rhythm to express ideas and feelings in few words.

- **Line** and **stanza**: a poem is written in lines; a group of lines is a stanza. A two-line stanza is a *couplet*, four lines a *quatrain*.
- **Rhyme**: words that end with the same sound (*light / night*). **End rhyme** is at the ends of lines. The **rhyme scheme** is shown with letters: a poem whose lines 1 and 3 rhyme and lines 2 and 4 rhyme is *abab*.
- **Rhythm and meter**: the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. *Iambic* meter goes da-DUM (*the SUN is UP*).
- **Free verse** has no regular rhyme or meter.
- **Imagery**: words that appeal to the senses — sight, sound, smell, taste, touch. *The smell of rice steaming in a clay pot.*
- **Figurative language**: simile, metaphor, personification, hyperbole and others.
- **Tone** is the poet's attitude toward the subject (*hopeful, angry, playful*). **Mood** is the feeling the reader gets (*peaceful, sad, tense*).
- **Speaker**: the voice in the poem, which is not always the poet.

Common forms: a **sonnet** has 14 lines; a **haiku** has three lines of 5, 7 and 5 syllables; a **narrative poem** tells a story; a **lyric poem** expresses personal feelings.

## Elements of drama

A **drama** (play) is a story written to be performed by actors.

- **Script**: the written text. It has **dialogue** (what characters say) and **stage directions** (instructions, often in italics or brackets, about actions, setting, lighting and how lines are spoken).
- **Acts and scenes**: a play is divided into acts, and acts into scenes. A new scene often means a new time or place.
- **Cast of characters**: the list of characters at the start.
- **Monologue**: a long speech by one character to others. **Soliloquy**: a character speaks their thoughts aloud alone, as in Hamlet's "To be or not to be." **Aside**: a short remark to the audience that other characters don't hear.
- **Dramatic irony**: the audience knows something the characters don't.
- Plays follow the same plot structure as stories: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and resolution.
- **Tragedy** ends unhappily, often because of the hero's flaw; **comedy** is light and ends happily.

When reading a play, picture the stage: read the stage directions closely, and notice what each character wants and what stands in their way.
