# Claims, Evidence and Argumentative Writing

An **argumentative essay** takes a position on a debatable issue and supports it with reasons and evidence, so the reader accepts the position or at least takes it seriously.

## Parts of an argument

- **Claim**: the writer's position, stated clearly. It must be debatable — someone could reasonably disagree. *Schools should ban single-use plastics in their canteens.* (Not a claim: *Plastic is made from petroleum* — that's a fact.)
- **Reasons**: why the claim is true. *Single-use plastics pollute waterways; reusable options are affordable; schools teach by example.*
- **Evidence**: proof for each reason — facts, statistics, examples, expert statements, results of studies. Name your source: *According to the Department of Environment and Natural Resources…*
- **Warrant / reasoning**: explains how the evidence supports the reason. Don't just drop a statistic; say why it matters.
- **Counterclaim**: an opposing view stated fairly. *Some argue that reusable containers are inconvenient for busy students.*
- **Rebuttal**: your response showing why the counterclaim is weaker. *However, a school that sells reusable cups at cost and offers washing stations removes most of that inconvenience.*

Addressing the other side makes your argument more convincing, not weaker.

## Structure of the essay

1. **Introduction**: hook the reader (a surprising fact, question or short story), give background on the issue, and end with your **thesis statement** — the claim plus your main reasons.
2. **Body paragraphs**: one reason per paragraph. Start with a topic sentence, give evidence, explain it, and link back to the claim.
3. **Counterclaim and rebuttal paragraph**: present the strongest opposing view and answer it.
4. **Conclusion**: restate the thesis in new words, summarize the reasons, and end with a call to action or a look at what's at stake.

## Strong vs. weak support

- Strong: specific, recent, from a reliable source, relevant to the reason.
- Weak: personal feelings only, "everybody knows," one example treated as proof of everything, or emotional appeals with no facts.

Use a formal tone, avoid "I think" (just state the claim), and connect ideas with transitions such as *furthermore, in contrast, as a result, therefore*.
