# Fragments, Run-ons and Parallel Structure

## Sentence fragments

A **fragment** is a group of words punctuated like a sentence but missing a subject, a verb, or a complete thought.
- *Running to catch the bus.* (no subject or complete verb)
- *Because the road was flooded.* (a dependent clause alone)
- *For example, the mangroves in Bohol.* (no verb)

Fix it by adding the missing part or joining it to a nearby sentence:
- *I was running to catch the bus.*
- *Classes were suspended because the road was flooded.*

## Run-on sentences and comma splices

A **run-on** (fused sentence) joins two independent clauses with no punctuation: *I finished my project I submitted it online.*

A **comma splice** joins them with only a comma: *I finished my project, I submitted it online.*

Four ways to fix both:
1. Make two sentences: *I finished my project. I submitted it online.*
2. Use a comma + coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS): *I finished my project, and I submitted it online.*
3. Use a semicolon: *I finished my project; I submitted it online.*
4. Make one clause dependent: *After I finished my project, I submitted it online.*

Words like *however* and *therefore* are not conjunctions, so a comma before them is not enough: *The test was hard; however, I passed.*

## Parallel structure

**Parallelism** means using the same grammatical form for items in a list or pair. It makes writing balanced and easy to read.

- Not parallel: *She likes swimming, to read, and dance.*
- Parallel: *She likes **swimming**, **reading** and **dancing**.* or *She likes **to swim**, **to read** and **to dance**.*

- Not parallel: *The job requires patience, being creative and that you work hard.*
- Parallel: *The job requires **patience**, **creativity** and **hard work**.*

Keep parts parallel after paired words (*both … and, either … or, not only … but also*): *She is **not only** smart **but also** kind* (not *not only smart but also she is kind*).

Comparisons also need parallel forms: *Walking is easier than **running*** (not *than to run*).

## Other common errors

- **Dangling modifier**: *Walking to school, the rain started.* (The rain wasn't walking.) → *Walking to school, I got caught in the rain.*
- **Misplaced modifier**: *She almost ate the whole pizza* vs. *She ate almost the whole pizza.* Put the modifier next to the word it describes.
- **Shifts in tense or person**: keep the same tense and point of view unless there's a reason to change.
