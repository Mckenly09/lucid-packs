# Figures of Speech

A **figure of speech** uses words in a special way, not with their plain literal meaning, to create a picture, a feeling or emphasis. Poets and writers use them often.

## Comparisons

- **Simile** compares two unlike things using *like* or *as*. *Her smile is **like** the morning sun. He is **as** busy **as** a bee.*
- **Metaphor** compares two unlike things directly, without *like* or *as*. *The classroom **was** a zoo. Time **is** a thief.*
- **Personification** gives human qualities to animals, objects or ideas. *The wind **whispered** through the trees. The old jeepney **groaned** up the hill.*

## Exaggeration and understatement

- **Hyperbole** is extreme exaggeration for effect, not meant literally. *I've told you a **million times**. My bag weighs a **ton**.*
- **Understatement** makes something seem smaller or less important than it is. *Saying "it's a bit wet" during a flood.*

## Sound

- **Onomatopoeia** uses words that imitate sounds: *buzz, splash, bang, sizzle, tik-tak*.
- **Alliteration** repeats the first consonant sound in nearby words: ***S**ally **s**ells **s**eashells.*
- **Assonance** repeats vowel sounds: *the r**ai**n in Sp**ai**n*.

## Other common figures

- **Irony** says the opposite of what is meant, or shows a result opposite to what is expected. *A fire station burning down.*
- **Oxymoron** puts two opposite words together: *deafening silence, bittersweet, jumbo shrimp*.
- **Apostrophe** speaks directly to someone absent or to a thing: *"O Death, where is thy sting?"*
- **Metonymy** uses something closely related to stand for the thing: *Malacañang announced a holiday* (Malacañang = the President's office).
- **Synecdoche** uses a part for the whole: *All hands on deck* (hands = sailors).

## How to identify one

Ask: is this literally true? If not, what two things are being linked, and what does the link make you feel or see? *Like/as* means simile; a direct "is" comparison means metaphor; a non-human thing acting human means personification.
