# Subject-Verb Agreement

A verb must **agree** with its subject in number. A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb.

## The basic rule

In the present tense, a singular verb usually ends in -s; a plural verb does not. (This is the opposite of nouns, where -s makes a plural.)
- *The dog barks. The dogs bark.*
- *My sister is a nurse. My sisters are nurses.*
- *He has a bike. They have bikes.*

*I* and *you* always take the plural form: *I walk, you walk* (not *I walks*).

To find the subject, ignore phrases between the subject and the verb:
- *The box of mangoes **is** heavy.* (The subject is *box*, not *mangoes*.)
- *One of my friends **lives** in Davao.* (The subject is *one*.)

## Compound subjects

- Subjects joined by **and** are plural: *Ana and Ben **are** classmates.*
- With **or** / **nor**, the verb agrees with the subject closer to it: *Neither the teacher nor the students **were** late. Either the students or the teacher **is** coming.*

## Tricky subjects

- **Indefinite pronouns** such as *everyone, everybody, someone, nobody, each, either, neither* are singular: *Everyone **is** here. Each of the boys **has** a ticket.*
- *Both, few, many, several* are plural: *Many **were** absent.*
- **Collective nouns** (*team, class, family, group*) are usually singular when the group acts as one: *The team **practices** every day.*
- Some nouns end in -s but are singular: *news, mathematics, physics*. *The news **is** good.*
- Amounts of time, money or distance taken as one unit are singular: *Ten pesos **is** enough. Two hours **is** a long time.*
- In sentences beginning with *there is / there are*, the subject comes after the verb: *There **is** a book on the table. There **are** three books on the table.*
