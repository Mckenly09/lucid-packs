# Text Types and Structures

Writers organize texts in different ways depending on their **purpose**: to entertain, to inform or explain, to persuade, or to describe. Recognizing the type and structure helps you read faster and take better notes.

## Main text types

- **Narrative**: tells a story with characters, setting and plot. Short stories, novels, personal accounts. Purpose: to entertain or share an experience.
- **Expository (informational)**: explains facts and ideas without the writer's opinion. Textbooks, news reports, encyclopedia entries, how-to guides. Purpose: to inform.
- **Persuasive / argumentative**: tries to convince the reader. Editorials, speeches, ads, opinion essays. Purpose: to persuade.
- **Descriptive**: paints a picture with sensory details. Travel writing, character sketches. Purpose: to describe.
- **Procedural**: gives steps to do something. Recipes, manuals, science procedures.

## Structures of expository texts

Each structure has **signal words** that help you recognize it.

- **Description / list**: gives features or characteristics. Signal words: *for example, such as, characteristics, includes, also*.
- **Sequence / chronological order**: events or steps in order. Signal words: *first, next, then, after, finally, in 1898, before*.
- **Compare and contrast**: similarities and differences. Signal words: *similarly, likewise, both, unlike, however, whereas, on the other hand*.
- **Cause and effect**: why something happens and its results. Signal words: *because, since, therefore, as a result, consequently, led to, due to*.
- **Problem and solution**: a problem and one or more ways to solve it. Signal words: *the problem is, one solution, to solve this, as a result, the answer*.

## Using structure when reading and writing

- **Reading**: identify the purpose first, then the structure. Use a matching graphic organizer: a timeline for sequence, a Venn diagram for compare-contrast, a cause-and-effect chain, or a problem-solution chart.
- **Writing**: choose the structure that fits your purpose. To explain how typhoons form, use sequence and cause-effect; to compare two phones, use compare-contrast; to propose fixing a school problem, use problem-solution.

Many texts mix structures. A news article about a flood may describe the damage, explain its causes and end with solutions.
